Joseph Smith delivered the discourse on Sunday afternoon, 7 April 1844, in the outdoor grove near the unfinished Nauvoo temple, as a funeral sermon for King Follett, who had died in a well accident on 9 March (Smith, pp. 15, 28, 76). The event lasted more than two hours, but no shorthand stenographer or authorial script fixed its language; the surviving records are longhand reports of an oral event (Smith, pp. 13–15, 28). Smith distinguishes “aural audits,” which attempt fragments of close verbal capture, from “content audits,” which more openly compress and paraphrase, while warning that neither category yields a complete transcript (Smith, pp. 13–15).

Four contemporary manuscript reports form the surviving eyewitness record. Conference clerks Thomas Bullock and William Clayton wrote as Smith spoke and produced the two substantial aural audits; their reports have comparatively high verbal agreement but remain independent and incomplete (Smith, pp. 28, 58–61, 76–78). Willard Richards, Smith's private secretary and historian, entered a much briefer, compressed first-order report in Smith's journal (Smith, pp. 28, 58, 61, 78–79). Wilford Woodruff's journal account is a longer second-order report, apparently expanded from rough notes and memory; it can preserve vivid content while standing at a greater compositional distance from the event (Smith, pp. 58–60, 79; JSP, “Discourse, 7 April 1844, as Reported by Wilford Woodruff,” historical introduction). These four texts overlap extensively but differ in wording, sequence, detail, and coverage. Their sum may preserve the sermon's themes with some fidelity, but none can be treated as the sermon itself (Smith, pp. 13–15, 28).

The first received text was made almost immediately. On 10 April the apostles directed Bullock to prepare the conference minutes; between 23 and 26 April he fused his report with Clayton's, normally using his own as the narrative spine and drawing on Clayton for detail and clarification (Smith, pp. 73, 76, 80). Woodruff's and Richards's reports were not sources for this work (Smith, pp. 74, 80). Publication was delayed by printer illness, the killings of Joseph and Hyrum Smith on 27 June, and the succession crisis; the composite appeared under John Taylor's editorship in the issue of the *Times and Seasons* dated 15 August 1844, though printed in September (Smith, pp. 73–74, 80). The published text regularized grammar, expanded contractions, and reduced false starts and repetitions; it also combined parallel clauses and standardized some Hebrew forms (Smith, pp. 74, 76, 291 nn. 48–49). It is thus a crucial witness to reception, but not a fifth report of the spoken discourse.

That composite became only the first layer. In October 1855, Historian's Office clerk Jonathan Grimshaw combined the 1845 *Voice of Truth* redaction with the Woodruff and Richards reports in a new draft, GM0; George A. Smith, Bullock, and Brigham Young edited the material into GM1 (Smith, pp. 80, 133, 137). In April 1856 Robert L. Campbell copied GM1 into the *Manuscript History of the Church* (Smith, pp. 80, 133). This Utah text drew material from reports unavailable to Bullock in 1844 and also revised the inherited wording. It circulated through the *Deseret News* in 1857, the *Millennial Star* in 1861, and *The Contributor* in 1883 (Smith, pp. 80–81, 133–34). B. H. Roberts annotated the *Millennial Star* form in the *Improvement Era* in 1909 (Smith, pp. 81, 134, 170). After controversy over teachings on uncreated intelligence and the resurrection of children, the First Presidency stopped *Liahona* reprints and ordered the sermon removed from volume 6 of *History of the Church* in 1912 (Smith, pp. 81–83, 155, 192–97). Joseph Fielding Smith restored a Roberts-derived text in *Teachings of the Prophet Joseph Smith* in 1938 and incorporated it into the revised *History of the Church* in 1952 (Smith, pp. 82–83, 232–33).

Modern editors returned to the manuscripts but made different kinds of edition. Stan Larson's 1978 “newly amalgamated text” sought a readable reconstruction from all four reports, extending the long tradition of composing one discourse from multiple witnesses (Smith, pp. 83, 242). Andrew F. Ehat and Lyndon W. Cook's *The Words of Joseph Smith* (1980) instead made the contemporary reports available in a scholarly compilation and printed the King Follett records in parallel (Smith, pp. 83, 263). The present edition follows the latter documentary impulse more strictly: it keeps the reports distinct, aligns them without collapsing them, and places the *Times and Seasons* beside them so that readers can inspect the first act of amalgamation rather than inherit it invisibly.
