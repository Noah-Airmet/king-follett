## B — Thomas Bullock

**Document and role.** Thomas Bullock, *Minutes, 6–9 Apr. 1844*, pp. 14–22, Historian's Office, General Church Minutes, Church History Library; handwriting of Thomas Bullock. Bullock attended the conference specifically as a reporter and recorded the discourse as Joseph Smith spoke.[^jsp-b]

Smith classifies Bullock's and Clayton's records as the two longhand aural audits and judges them likely closer to the spoken archetype than the more retrospective reports, while stressing that even an aural audit is neither complete nor verbatim (Smith, pp. 13–15, 59–60, 76). Bullock's report is the fullest continuous eyewitness record and serves as this edition's base, but its compressed constructions and occasional apparent slips remain visible rather than being silently repaired.

## W — Wilford Woodruff

**Document and role.** Wilford Woodruff, *Journal*, vol. 5, 1 Jan. 1843–31 Dec. 1844, pp. [133]–[140], Wilford Woodruff, Journals and Papers, Church History Library; handwriting of Wilford Woodruff. Woodruff was apparently not assigned to report the conference. The Joseph Smith Papers describes the surviving journal account as probably a fair copy or refinement of rough notes taken while Smith spoke; Woodruff later recalled writing on the crown of his hat while standing in the congregation.[^jsp-w]

Smith calls Woodruff's text an expanded, second-order audit, a digest of notes and memory, and observes that it has less verbal agreement with Bullock and Clayton than those two have with each other (Smith, pp. 58–60, 79). Its more continuous prose can preserve fuller thematic summaries and striking images, but that fluency should not be mistaken for a verbatim transcript.

## R — Willard Richards

**Document and role.** Joseph Smith, *Journal, 1842–1844*, book 4, pp. [67]–[71], JS Collection, Church History Library; handwriting of Willard Richards. Richards was Smith's private secretary and historian and keeper of his journal; he recorded the sermon as Smith spoke.[^jsp-r]

The Joseph Smith Papers describes the record as shorter, less detailed, and at times cryptic.[^jsp-r] Smith likewise calls it a brief first-order audit that often witnesses—and sometimes diverges from—the two longer aural audits (Smith, pp. 28, 58, 61, 78–79). Its telegraphic form limits context, yet it sometimes preserves names, terms, or compact clauses absent from the other reports.

## C — William Clayton

**Document and role.** William Clayton, *Minutes, 6–8 Apr. 1844*, pp. 11–18, Historian's Office, General Church Minutes, Church History Library; handwriting of William Clayton. Clayton was Smith's private clerk and recorded the discourse as Smith spoke; the account ends abruptly before the final part of the sermon.[^jsp-c]

Smith treats Clayton with Bullock as one of the two primary longhand aural audits and notes their comparatively high verbal agreement (Smith, pp. 15, 28, 58–61, 76, 78). Clayton frequently supplies an independent parallel to Bullock and sometimes preserves clauses or formulations absent from it. The abrupt ending, however, makes silence in the closing sections a feature of the document's coverage, not evidence that the material was not spoken.

## T — *Times and Seasons*

**Document and role.** “Conference Minutes,” *Times and Seasons* 5, no. 15 (15 Aug. 1844): 612–617, Nauvoo, Illinois. T is not an eyewitness report. Thomas Bullock and William Clayton amalgamated their notes into the published version four months after the sermon and two months after Joseph Smith's death.[^jsp-t]

Smith describes Bullock's own report as the composite's spine, supplemented and clarified from Clayton, and states that Woodruff and Richards were not used (Smith, pp. 73–76, 80). The resulting text regularizes spoken syntax, removes much oral roughness, and sometimes fuses parallel clauses into a statement no single eyewitness wrote (Smith, pp. 74, 76). T matters as the beginning of the received textual tradition, but never as independent evidence for what was spoken.
